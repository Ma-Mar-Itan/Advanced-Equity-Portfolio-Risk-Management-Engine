# Research foundations

Use this reference when explaining why the skill recommends a speaking or reporting practice. Apply the principles rather than quoting the sources at length.

## Audience, purpose, and narrative

- MIT’s Communication Lab recommends defining the audience, purpose, and central message before opening slide software. It proposes an hourglass narrative: begin with the broader problem, move into the specific work, and end with its wider meaning. It also recommends grouping results by the points they support rather than by the order in which the analysis was performed. [MIT: Structuring a Slide Presentation](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/nse/commkit/structuring-a-slide-presentation/)
- Toastmasters advises technical briefings to state their purpose in one or two sentences, match technical depth to the audience, and organize the briefing into an introduction, main points, and conclusion. [Toastmasters: Delivering Technical Briefings](https://www.toastmasters.org/resources/public-speaking-tips/delivering-technical-briefings)
- Stanford’s Oral Communication Program emphasizes using PowerPoint only where a visual explanation helps, keeping the presenter rather than the slides as the focal point, and making verbal transitions explain why the slides appear in their chosen order. [Stanford: Visuals and PowerPoint](https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/visuals-and-powerpoint)

## Slide and chart communication

- MIT recommends one message per slide, titles that state the message, simplified figures, annotations, and deliberate control of information delivery. [MIT: Slide Design](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/nse/commkit/slide-design/)
- Microsoft recommends readable fonts, high contrast, minimal text, comprehensible chart labels, rehearsal, time control, and testing the actual equipment. It also warns against reading the slides. [Microsoft: Tips for creating and delivering an effective presentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/tips-for-creating-and-delivering-an-effective-presentation)
- W3C guidance notes that audiences cannot efficiently read dense slides while listening. It recommends limited text, sufficiently large visuals, high contrast, plain language, verbal description of important graphics, and pauses for processing. [W3C WAI: Making Events Accessible](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/)
- Stanford advises using one slide for one idea, displaying only evidence the speaker will discuss, and defining technical terms and acronyms. [Stanford: Visuals and PowerPoint](https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/visuals-and-powerpoint)

## Risk-reporting obligations

- Basel’s risk-reporting principles require accurate, comprehensive, clear, concise, useful, and recipient-specific reports. They call for a balance of risk data, analysis, interpretation, and qualitative explanation. Reports should identify concentrations, connect risk to limits or tolerance, include stress and forward-looking scenarios, and propose action where appropriate. [BIS: Risk data aggregation and risk reporting](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/standard/srp/36/inforce/2019-12-15/published/2019-12-15)
- Basel describes stress testing as a forward-looking risk-management input to business decisions. Users need to understand scenario relevance, risk coverage, assumptions, and model limitations. [BIS: Stress testing principles](https://www.bis.org/publications/201810-guidelines-stress-testing-principles)
- The Federal Reserve’s revised 2026 model-risk guidance emphasizes intended use, data and assumptions, outcome analysis, backtesting, ongoing monitoring, limitations, effective challenge, and complementary analysis. A speech should therefore explain not just a model’s output but why it is fit for purpose and where it remains uncertain. [Federal Reserve: Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/guidance/supervisory-guidance-on-model-risk-management.htm)
- CFA Institute’s communication standard requires disclosure of the investment process, material risks and limitations, relevant factors, and the distinction between fact and opinion. Its VaR example specifically warns against describing VaR as more reliable than its assumptions allow. [CFA Institute: Standard V(B)](https://www.cfainstitute.org/standards/professionals/code-ethics-standards/standards-of-practice-v-b)
- CFA Institute lists major VaR limitations: sensitivity to model choices, underestimation of extreme events, liquidity and correlation risk, regime dependence, and the common error of interpreting VaR as a worst-case loss. [CFA Institute: Measuring and Managing Market Risk](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/measuring-managing-market-risk)

## Language and delivery

- The SEC’s plain-English guidance favors short sentences, concrete everyday language, active voice, descriptive headings, and tabular treatment of complex information. It discourages jargon, boilerplate, repetition, and legalistic explanations that hide the substance. [SEC: Plain English Disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/interps/legal/cfslb7a.htm)
- MIT’s EECS Communication Lab treats slides as visual aids rather than the talk itself. It recommends facing the audience, preparing for Q&A, repeating a question before answering, and using backup slides for technical detail. [MIT EECS: Slide Presentation](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/slideshow/)

## Synthesis for this project

For the QFI portfolio-risk talk, these sources support five operating rules:

1. Lead with the decision and loss consequence, then earn trust with methods and validation.
2. Show fewer results, but interpret each result and connect it to a management question.
3. State the model configuration and limitations with the estimate.
4. Use backtesting, stress testing, and attribution as complementary challenges to VaR.
5. End with a monitoring or action recommendation rather than a generic summary.
